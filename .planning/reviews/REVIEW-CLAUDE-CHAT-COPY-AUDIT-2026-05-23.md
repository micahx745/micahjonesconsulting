1. Market Landscape: Where You Sit

The fractional operator market exploded between 2022 and 2026. LinkedIn profiles mentioning fractional leadership went from 2,000 to over 110,000. That growth created two tiers: the marketplace tier (Toptal, Bolster, GoFractional) where operators are commodities sorted by rate, and the boutique tier where operators position as named practices with a body of work. You belong in the second tier. The question is whether your site reads that way.

Most fractional operator sites in 2026 fall into three copy patterns:

The agency pattern. "We help [segment] achieve [outcome] through [method]." This is the most common and the most forgettable. Chief Outsiders, GoFractional, most CMO-for-hire sites.

The credential stack. A LinkedIn summary turned into a website. Lists of logos, years of experience, titles held. No narrative. No stakes. Reads like a resume with better fonts.

The operator-as-author pattern. Rare. The site reads like the operator wrote it, not their agency. First person. Specific verbs. Named outcomes. Rauno Freiberg's craft posts. Linear's README. Stripe Press marginalia. Your site aims for this tier.

Your site is currently a 70/30 split between the third pattern and the second. The case studies are strong operator-as-author work. The home page drifts toward credential stacking in places. The services page has agency-pattern echoes in its tier descriptions. The audit below names each instance.

Voice Reference Comparison

Against the five voice references you named, here is where you land:

Rauno Freiberg (rauno.me/craft): His register is reflective, technical, and precise. Sentences like "if an animation didn't perform well, felt pompous, or out of rhythm relative to the page, we didn't build it" carry conviction through restraint. Your case studies match this register. Your home page doesn't.

Linear README (linear.app/readme): Narrative-driven, almost literary. Three-act structure. Short declarative sentences. "This is not a manifesto. This is not a codex." Your footer CTA and hero approach this energy but don't sustain it.

Stripe Press: Marginalia and specimen copy. Every word earns its place. Your best lines ("Strategy and software, shipped by the same pair of hands") sit here. Your worst lines ("Real work in real users' hands") don't.

The Gentlewoman / Pinkerton Zweck / Klim: Editorial credits and specimen pages. Terse. Your eyebrow copy ("Independent operator · Oakland, CA") nails this. Your service marquee doesn't.

Engagement Tier Presentation

The advisory/project/retainer/embedded framework is the right structure. Most fractional operators in 2026 present two or three tiers. Four tiers with explicit scope, duration, and first-month deliverables is premium positioning. The risk is that 12 cells of copy can read as a menu if the verbs aren't sharp. Yours are inconsistent—some cells read as boutique, others as staffing-agency boilerplate.

2. Priority Surface Critique

2.1 Hero H1 + Rotating Word

Verdict: Mediocre. The structure works; the words don't all earn their place.

Current: "I build the [PRODUCT. / PIPELINE. / LAUNCH. / SYSTEM.]"

Failure mode: "Pipeline" and "system" are abstract nouns that could belong to any consultant's site. They don't name what a $200K buyer actually purchases. "Product" and "launch" are sharper because they name real artifacts. The rotating pattern is good—it signals range without claiming omniscience—but the word list needs to be 4-for-4 specific, not 2-for-4.

Alternatives

A: "I build the [PRODUCT. / POSITION. / LAUNCH. / ENGINE.]"

"Position" names the Guardicore work directly. "Engine" names the content/evidence systems. Both are artifacts a buyer can picture.

B: "I build the [PRODUCT. / NARRATIVE. / LAUNCH. / INFRASTRUCTURE.]"

"Narrative" names what carries the enterprise sale. "Infrastructure" names what Ordani and the AI work share.

C: "I build the [PRODUCT. / REPOSITIONING. / LAUNCH. / EVIDENCE.]"

Most specific. "Repositioning" names the Guardicore outcome. "Evidence" names the SurveyMonkey outcome. Risks being too narrow for an AI buyer.

Ship this: Option A. "Position" and "engine" are concrete enough for a buyer to picture, broad enough for three service lines. "Pipeline" and "system" should go.

2.2 Hero Sub-line

Verdict: Working, with one soft spot.

Current: "Strategy and software, shipped by the same pair of hands. I build go-to-market for clients — and products with real users."

The first sentence is strong. "Same pair of hands" is specific, physical, memorable. The second sentence repeats the H1's claim without adding evidence. "Products with real users" is vague—Ordani has 14 practices and hundreds of users. Name the number.

Alternatives

A: "Strategy and software, shipped by the same pair of hands. Go-to-market for enterprise clients. A live product with hundreds of users."

B: "Strategy and software, shipped by the same pair of hands. Enterprise GTM and a product in private beta with fourteen practices."

Ship this: Option B. It names the exact scale without overclaiming. A $200K buyer reads "fourteen practices" and knows this person actually ships.

2.3 Section Titles Across Home

Verdict: Mixed. One strong, one weak, one fine.

"Strategy that ships, not slides."

Working. The opposition structure earns its place. "Ships, not slides" is concrete and implies a point of view about the industry. Keep.

"Shipped."

Mediocre. As a one-word section title it's trying to do the work of a full sentence through brevity alone. Compare to how Rauno titles his case study: "What will you ship?" One word feels more like a sticker than a section header. It doesn't tell the buyer what they're about to see.

A: "Work that shipped."

B: "Three engagements. Named outcomes."

C: "The receipts."

Ship this: Option A. "Work that shipped" preserves the verb energy, adds enough context for a scanner, and sits well above three engagement cards.

"LET'S BUILD →"

Not working. "Let's build" is a startup-culture trope. It's the most common CTA on indie operator sites. It doesn't earn its display-scale treatment. A $200K buyer doesn't want to "build" with you—they want to hire you to own a problem.

A: "START A CONVERSATION →"

B: "BOOK THE FIRST CALL →"

C: "NAME THE PROBLEM →"

Ship this: Option C. "Name the problem" reframes the CTA as the buyer's action, not yours. It implies you're listening, not pitching. It sits well in display type and differentiates from every "Let's chat" footer on the internet.

03 — Frontier AI engineering

Current: "Production architecture and orchestration for founders shipping AI-native software."

Undercooked. "Production architecture and orchestration" is a description of a category, not a point of view. Every AI consultancy says this. In a market where OpenAI's Frontier Alliances just handed McKinsey and Accenture the enterprise AI deployment narrative, a solo operator needs a sharper differentiator than "production-grade."

A: "The eval infrastructure, deployment pipelines, and failure-mode work that turns a frontier model into a product people use."

B: "Eval infrastructure and prompt deployment for founders whose AI has to work in production, not in a notebook."

C: "The engineering between the model and the user. Eval, orchestration, retrieval, deployment."

Ship this: Option C. "The engineering between the model and the user" names the gap that founders actually feel. The four-noun list (eval, orchestration, retrieval, deployment) is specific enough to signal competence without overclaiming.

2.5 Tier Descriptions (12 cells)

Verdict: Inconsistent. Some read as boutique; others read as staffing-agency scope docs.

The strongest cells are the Project tiers. "30 customer interviews + 50-call sales-call analysis + interim category-shift memo by week 4" is excellent—it names exact artifacts with exact timelines. A buyer can picture the work.

The weakest cells are the Advisory tiers. "Strategic review of current positioning + monthly sounding board" could appear on any fractional CMO's site. "Sounding board" is soft. Across all three services, the Advisory tier uses near-identical language patterns.

Specific Fixes

Advisory tiers: Replace "sounding board" with what actually happens in those hours. "Monthly positioning review + narrative pressure-test against live deals" is sharper.

Embedded tiers: "Acting head of [X]" is strong framing. Keep. But "full ownership of build velocity + product quality" is abstract. Name what the buyer sees in month one: "First major feature shipped + security posture documented + sprint cadence running."

Retainer tiers: "Month-to-month, 6-month minimum" is good transparency. But "continuous tuning of the message" is vague. Try: "Monthly narrative iteration tied to pipeline data + quarterly repositioning review."

2.6 Ordani Lede

Verdict: Working as description, not as pitch.

Current: "HIPAA-grade practice management for birth workers — built end-to-end by one operator. Row-level encryption inside Supabase RLS, two outside security reviews, every read logged. 14 practices, hundreds of users; eight active after six months. Zero churn."

This reads like a technical spec with metrics attached. It describes what Ordani is. It doesn't say why anyone should care. A healthtech founder reading this section doesn't learn what problem Ordani solves for the birth workers—they learn how it's built. Lead with the problem, follow with the build.

A: "Birth workers run their practices on group chats and paper intakes. HIPAA is the law; compliance is impossible without real infrastructure. I built it. Row-level encryption, two outside security reviews, fourteen practices in private beta. Zero churn."

B: "The first HIPAA-compliant practice management built for doulas and midwives. Solo build on Next.js + Supabase. Row-level encryption, two outside security reviews. Fourteen practices; eight still active at six months. Zero churn."

Ship this: Option A. Leading with the problem ("Birth workers run their practices on group chats") gives the reader a reason to care before the build specs arrive. It also echoes the operator voice—"I built it" does more work than "built end-to-end by one operator."

2.7 Frontier AI: NDA Framing

Verdict: Working, barely. One revision needed.

4. Overall Score and Final Recommendations

Site Copy Score: 7 out of 10

Boutique consulting practice, not indie hustler—but with patches of indie-hustler copy still attached. The case studies are 9/10 work. The services page is 7/10. The home page is 6/10. The gap is between the top-of-funnel copy (home hero, section titles, marquee) and the deep-funnel copy (case study bodies, service tier details). A buyer who only sees the home page gets a different register than a buyer who reads the Guardicore case study.

What would move the score to 9: Fix the hero rotating words (pipeline → position, system → engine). Kill "LET'S BUILD." Cut the claim-without-evidence sentences ("Real work in real users' hands," "Named institutional customers"). Sharpen the Advisory tier language across all three services. Add one missing surface (see below).

Single Line to Change First

Section: Footer CTA. Current: "LET'S BUILD →". Replacement: "NAME THE PROBLEM →". This is the line that appears at the bottom of every page. It's the last thing a buyer reads before deciding to click or leave. "Let's build" sounds like a hackathon tagline. "Name the problem" sounds like a $200K operator who listens before acting.

One Missing Surface: A Principles or Method Section

The site has Services (what you do), Work (proof you did it), and a CTA. It's missing a short section—two or three paragraphs—that names how you think about the work. Not a manifesto. Not a philosophy page. Something closer to Linear's README in spirit but at 1/10th the length. A section called "How I work" or "Operating principles" with three to five short statements. Examples:

"I don't present options. I present a recommendation and the evidence behind it."

"Every engagement has a named artifact in the first month. If it doesn't ship, the engagement isn't working."

"I work in first person. The work carries one signature, not a team's."

This surface does two things: it gives the buyer language to repeat internally when they're selling you to their CEO, and it differentiates you from the agency model more clearly than any credential ever will. The best boutique practices have this. Your site doesn't yet.
