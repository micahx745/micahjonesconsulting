# Why isn't my landing page converting?

The page is live, the layout is clean, and people land on it and leave. You have rewritten it three times and still cannot say in one sentence what it does.

Once the plumbing checks out, it is usually that sentence, not the layout. A landing page converts when the right people arrive, the signup works, and a stranger can tell in five seconds who it is for, what it takes away, and what to do next. The rest of this post is that check, then the sentence, the test, and the line where the page loses everyone else.

## How do I tell whether the problem is traffic, copy, or the signup itself?

Rule out the plumbing before you touch a word. Four checks, none longer than ten minutes.

First, who is arriving. Look at the referring sites and the search queries. If the visits come from a search for something you do not do, or from a directory that sends anyone, the page is not failing; the wrong people are reading it. Second, do the signup yourself on a phone, on mobile data, with a fresh email address. A form that rejects a valid input, a button under a cookie bar, or a confirmation email that never lands all look like a copy problem in the numbers. Third, load the page on that phone and count the seconds; a slow or jumpy page loses the visitor before the headline gets its five seconds. Fourth, check that the price and the next step are visible without hunting.

When all four pass and people still leave, the words are the problem.

## Why do visitors leave without signing up?

Because the page explains the product and the visitor came with a problem. A stranger gives it five seconds and asks one thing: is this for me? If the first line answers with a feature, they go.

You know the product inside out, so the page reads clearly to you. The visitor knows nothing, and the first line has to carry all of that context on its own. "AI-powered workflow automation for invoices" is a feature. "Get paid on time without the awkward email" is a problem and a result. The first line is about them, not about the product.

## How do I say what my app does in one sentence?

Fill three slots: the person, the situation, the result. "For freelancers who chase late invoices, this sends a reminder that sounds like you and gets the invoice paid." That sentence skips most of the features. It names who it is for, what hurts, and what changes.

Write it before you touch anything else on the page. Specific beats big. "Manage your whole client cycle" promises everything and describes nobody, so nobody sees themselves in it.

## What should the headline promise?

The result, in the visitor's words, with no product name and no category in it. The headline is the one sentence cut down to the part they came for. "Get paid on time without the awkward email" is a headline. "Automated invoice reminders for freelancers" is a catalogue entry.

The line under it names the person and the mechanism: "For freelancers who chase late invoices. It sends the follow-up in your voice, so you stay friendly and still get paid." That pair is the whole promise. Everything below exists to prove it.

## What should go below the headline?

Proof of the promise, then the three steps, then the one button. A visitor who believes the headline asks three things in order: is this really for me, what do I have to do, and what happens first?

Proof means showing the result concretely, not arguing for it: a screenshot of the reminder as a client receives it, a short clip of one going out, or a before and after of the inbox. That shows the thing works, which is all a page can show; whether anyone pays is a question for the counts later. Features come after, as support for the result, not as the reason to sign up. Then one line for the steps: "Connect your invoicing tool, pick one reminder template, and the follow-up goes out on its own." Then the button.

The whole page, top to bottom: the headline (the result), the line under it (the person and the mechanism), the proof (the result, shown), the three steps, the button. Copy that order onto a blank page and fill the slots before you open a design tool.

## How do I find the words my visitors already use?

Listen to one person describe the problem in their own terms and copy the phrasing without cleaning it up. If a freelancer says "I hate the awkward follow-up email," the page says "the awkward follow-up email," not "streamlined client communication." A visitor recognizes their own sentence faster than any sentence you could write for them.

Two places hold those words. The first is a five-minute call with the kind of person you want: ask them to describe the last time the problem happened, and write down the exact phrases. The second is the email or support message in which someone first asked for the thing you built. That request, more or less as written, is often the best headline you will get.

## How do I test whether my landing page is clear?

Run a five-second test with five people who have never seen the page. Show it for five seconds, close it, and ask one question: what do you think this is for? A good answer contains the person and the result. "Something about invoices" is a miss. "It chases late invoices for freelancers" is a hit.

Five people cannot tell you whether the page converts. They can tell you what it says to a stranger, and that is what you are testing. If most of them describe the problem you meant, the page is understood. If they describe something else, that is the misunderstanding to fix: change the headline first and run it again. Do this before any design work, and not with friends, who will read the product into the page for you.

## How do I know where the page loses people?

Watch recordings of visitors who landed and left. [Microsoft Clarity](https://clarity.microsoft.com) is free, and it records sessions and draws heatmaps of where people click, scroll and drop off. Install it, give it a few days of traffic, and watch five recordings of people who never reached the button. Find the point where the scrolling stops or the cursor drifts back to the top. That is the place to look first.

Maybe the headline worked and the "how it works" line introduced a term they did not know. Maybe they reached the price before they knew what they were paying for. The recording tells you where. The words still tell you why.

## Should I change the words or the design first?

Words first. The clearest button on the internet cannot rescue a headline that says "workflow automation platform." People leave because they cannot see themselves on the page, not because it lacks polish.

Change three things: the headline, the line under it, and the label on the main button. The button names the next step the person takes, not "Submit" and not "Get started." For the invoice example it reads "Send my first reminder." If the page is readable on a phone and the button is easy to tap, leave the design alone until the new words have been tested.

## How do I know if the landing page is converting?

Count completed target actions against the visits from each source, not visits alone. Pick a single goal that matches the promise: a signup, a started trial, or a question sent. If the promise is "get paid on time," the goal is the click that sets up the first reminder, and nothing else on the page counts. A search that matches the promise and produces no signups is a copy problem. A directory that sends anyone and produces no signups is a traffic problem, and no headline fixes it. Any analytics tool shows visits by referrer and can count one event; if you run none, [umami](https://github.com/umami-software/umami) is lightweight, privacy-friendly analytics that counts visits and the few events you tell it to.

Then check what people typed to find you. [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console/about) shows which queries put your page in Google results, with impressions and clicks for each. If the query is "invoice reminder for freelancers" and the headline says "client communication made simple," the page and the search do not share a vocabulary, and the visitor arrived with the search's. Those queries are evidence of the words people use, not a headline to paste in. Write the headline in that vocabulary and keep the promise yours.

## What should I do this week?

Run the four plumbing checks today, then write the one sentence: person, situation, result. Put the result in the headline and the person in the line under it. Run the five-second test with five strangers tomorrow. Install Clarity, and by Thursday watch five recordings of people who left. Change the one line where they stopped, then run the five-second test again. By Friday you will know whether the page says what you meant, which is more than the last three rewrites told you.
