# Why isn’t my landing page converting?

Your page works and the layout is clean, but people still land and leave. A forum post about this usually sounds like the quote above: “I keep rewriting my landing page and I still can’t say in one sentence what problem it actually solves.” That is the real problem, not the layout.

The answer is usually the words. Visitors cannot tell in one sentence who the page is for, what it does, and what they get. The rest of this post shows you how to fix that sentence and test it.

## Why isn’t my landing page converting?

Because the page explains the product and not the problem. A stranger gives it five seconds and asks one thing: “Is this for me?” If the first sentence answers with a feature, they leave.

You know the product inside out, so the page sounds clear to you. The visitor does not know the product. The page has to carry that context in one line. For example, “AI-powered workflow automation for invoices” is a feature. “Send a reminder that sounds like you and get paid without the awkward email” is a problem and a result. The first sentence can be about them, not about the product.

## How do I say what my app does in one sentence?

Fill in three slots: person, situation, result. “For freelancers who still chase late invoices, this app sends a reminder that sounds like you and gets paid without the awkward email.” That sentence does not describe every feature. It names the person, the pain, and the outcome.

Write that sentence before you touch the rest of the page. You are allowed to be specific at the cost of sounding big. The page that says “manage your whole client cycle” does not convert as well as the page that says “get paid on time without the awkward email.”

## What should the headline promise?

The headline should promise the result most people came for. It should not name the product or the category. If your product is for freelancers who hate chasing late invoices, the headline might be “Get paid on time without the awkward email.” The old headline, “Automated invoice reminders for freelancers,” describes the feature. The new one describes the outcome.

Under the headline, one line should anchor the result: “This app follows up on late invoices so you can stay friendly and still get paid.” That is the whole promise. The rest of the page exists to support it.

## What should go below the headline?

Below the headline, answer the next three questions in order: is this for me, what do I have to do, and what happens first? A short line that says “Connect your invoicing tool, set one reminder template, and the app does the follow-up” tells a busy freelancer the work is small and the benefit is specific.

Do not start with a wall of features. Start with proof that the first sentence is true. Then show the three steps it takes to get that result. Then ask for the one action.

## How do I find the words my visitors already use?

Listen to one person describe the problem in their own terms, then copy those words. Do not clean them up. If a freelancer says “I hate the awkward follow-up email,” then the page should say “the awkward follow-up email,” not “streamlined client communication.” Real words match the voice already in the visitor’s head.

One place to get those words is a five-minute call with the kind of person you want. Ask them to describe the last time the problem happened. Write down the exact phrases. Another place is the email or support question where someone first asked for the thing you built. That sentence often belongs on the page.

## How do I test whether the page communicates the product?

Run a five-second test. Show the page for five seconds, close it, and ask one question: “What do you think this is for?” The answer should contain the person and the result, not the phrase “some kind of platform.”

If they say “something about invoices,” that is not enough. If they say “it sends payment reminders for freelancers,” the sentence is working. Do this with five people before you do any design work. If three of them describe the same problem you meant, keep the copy. If they describe something else, change the headline first and run it again.

## How do I know where the page loses people?

Watch recordings of a few visitors who landed and left. Open Microsoft Clarity (VERIFY) at clarity.microsoft.com and watch five recordings. Clarity is free and shows a heatmap and session recording. Find the point where someone stops scrolling or moves back to the top. That is usually where the page stopped answering their next question.

Then fix the line at that point. Maybe the headline worked but the “how it works” sentence introduced a new term they did not know. Maybe they scrolled to the price and left because the value was not clear. The recording tells you where; the words still tell you why.

## Should I change the words or the design first?

Words first, because the clearest button in the world cannot fix a headline that says “workflow automation platform.” People leave when they cannot see themselves, not because the page lacks polish.

Change the headline, the one-sentence subhead, and the main button label. The button should be the next step the person takes, not “Submit.” For the invoice example, it becomes “Stop the awkward follow-up.” If the design is readable on a phone and the button is easy to tap, leave the design alone until the words have been tested.

## How do I know if the landing page is converting?

Count the one action on the page, not the visits. Set up a single goal: a visitor signs up, starts a trial, or asks a question. The goal should match the promise. If the promise is “get paid on time,” the button should say “Stop the awkward follow-up,” and clicking it is the conversion.

Check the words in Google Search Console (VERIFY) at search.google.com/search-console. Look at the queries that brought impressions. If the query is “invoice reminder for freelancers” and the headline says “client communication made simple,” the page and the search are not aligned. Use the search terms as headline material.

## What should I do this week?

Write the one sentence today using person, situation, result. Put it in the headline and the subhead. Run a five-second test with five people. Watch five recordings in Microsoft Clarity (VERIFY). Change the one line that people stopped at.

By Friday, you will know whether the page says what you think it says. That is the whole problem.
