# Why isn’t my landing page converting?

Your page works and the layout is clean, but people still land and leave. A forum post about this usually sounds like the quote above: “I keep rewriting my landing page and I still can’t say in one sentence what problem it actually solves.” That is the real problem, not the layout.

The answer is usually the words. Visitors cannot tell in one sentence who the page is for, what it does, and what they get. The rest of this post shows you how to fix that sentence and test it.

## Why isn’t my landing page converting?

Because the page explains the product and not the problem. A stranger gives it five seconds and asks one thing: “Is this for me?” If the first sentence answers with a feature, they leave.

You know the product inside out, so the page sounds clear to you. The visitor does not know the product. The page has to carry that context in one line. For example, “AI-powered workflow automation for invoices” is a feature. “Send a reminder that sounds like you and get paid without the awkward email” is a problem and a result. The first sentence can be about them, not about the product.

## How do I say what my app does in one sentence?

Fill in three slots: person, situation, result. “For freelancers who still chase late invoices, this app sends a reminder that sounds like you and gets paid without the awkward email.” That sentence does not describe every feature. It names the person, the pain, and the outcome.

Write that sentence before you touch the rest of the page. You are allowed to be specific at the cost of sounding big. The page that says “manage your whole client cycle” does not convert as well as the page that says “get paid on time without the awkward email.”

## What should the headline promise?

The headline should promise the result most people came for. It should not name the product or the category. If your product is for freelancers who hate chasing late invoices, the headline might be “Get paid on time without the awkward email.” The old headline, “Automated invoice reminders for freelancers,” describes the feature. The new one describes the outcome.

Under the headline, one line should anchor the result: “This app follows up on late invoices so you can stay friendly and still get paid.” That is the whole promise. The rest of the page exists to support it.

## What should go below the headline?

Below the headline, answer the next three questions in order: is this for me, what do I have to do, and what happens first? A short line that says “Connect your invoicing tool, set one reminder template, and the app does the follow-up” tells a busy freelancer the work is small and the benefit is specific.

Do not start with a wall of features. Start with proof that the first sentence is true. Then show the three steps it takes to get that result. Then ask for the one action.

## How do I find the words my visitors already use?

Listen to one person describe the problem in their own terms, then copy those words. Do not clean them up. If a freelancer says “I hate the awkward follow-up email,” then the page should say “the awkward follow-up email,” not “streamlined client communication.” Real words match the voice already in the visitor’s head.

One place to get those words is a five-minute call with the kind of person you want. Ask them to describe the last time the problem happened. Write down the exact phrases. Another place is the email or support question where someone first asked for the thing you built. That sentence often belongs on the page.

## How do I test whether the page communicates the product?

Run a five-second test. Show the page for five seconds, close it, and ask one question: “What do you think this is for?” The answer should contain the person and the result, not the phrase “some kind of platform.”

If they say “something about invoices,” that is not enough. If they say “it sends payment reminders for freelancers,” the sentence is working. Do this with five people before you do any design work. If three of them describe the same problem you meant, keep the copy. If they describe something else, change the headline first and run it again.

## How do I know where the page loses people?

Watch recordings of a few visitors who landed and left. Open Microsoft Clarity (VERIFY) at clarity.microsoft.com and watch five recordings. Clarity is free and shows a heatmap and session recording. Find the point where someone stops scrolling or moves back to the top. That is usually where the page stopped answering their next question.

Then fix the line at that point. Maybe the headline worked but the “how it works” sentence introduced a new term they did not know. Maybe they scrolled to the price and left because the value was not clear. The recording tells you where; the words still tell you why.

## Should I change the words or the design first?

Words first, because the clearest button in the world cannot fix a headline that says “workflow automation platform.” People leave when they cannot see themselves, not because the page lacks polish.

Change the headline, the one-sentence subhead, and the main button label. The button should be the next step the person takes, not “Submit.” For the invoice example, it becomes “Stop the awkward follow-up.” If the design is readable on a phone and the button is easy to tap, leave the design alone until the words have been tested.

## How do I know if the landing page is converting?

Count the one action on the page, not the visits. Set up a single goal: a visitor signs up, starts a trial, or asks a question. The goal should match the promise. If the promise is “get paid on time,” the button should say “Stop the awkward follow-up,” and clicking it is the conversion.

Check the words in Google Search Console (VERIFY) at search.google.com/search-console. Look at the queries that brought impressions. If the query is “invoice reminder for freelancers” and the headline says “client communication made simple,” the page and the search are not aligned. Use the search terms as headline material.

## What should I do this week?

Write the one sentence today using person, situation, result. Put it in the headline and the subhead. Run a five-second test with five people. Watch five recordings in Microsoft Clarity (VERIFY). Change the one line that people stopped at.

By Friday, you will know whether the page says what you think it says. That is the whole problem.

# How do I sell my app when I am a builder, not a salesperson?

Your app works. People who use it get a result. The problem is that the people who should use it do not know it exists.

Here is the answer; the rest is the mechanism. You sell by picking ten specific buyers, sending a message about one problem they already recognize, and asking for a short call. You do not need a sales personality. You need a list, a message, a follow-up sequence, and four numbers.

## Why can't I sell my product even though it works?

Because you are selling a product instead of a change. The product is too big to explain and too easy to ignore. The change is specific: before this, you spent Monday morning copying invoices by hand; after this, the export produces them before you finish coffee. If you cannot say the change in one sentence, you are not ready to sell yet.

Most builders who cannot sell have a working product aimed at a blurry person. They write to "small teams" or "busy operators." A buyer cannot find themselves in that. You need one person who does the job by hand today and has public evidence of it. That evidence is the doorway.

## Who should I contact first?

People who already do the job your app replaces, and who show the cost of doing it by hand. Ten names, not a market.

Look for evidence in public places: a forum thread asking how to automate the task, a job post for a person to run it, a template shared to manage it, or a support ticket that keeps repeating. If you cannot find ten people with that evidence, narrow the role or change the job you are targeting.

For a business buyer, use [LinkedIn Sales Navigator](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/sales-navigator) (VERIFY) to filter by title, company size, and industry. For email addresses, use [Hunter.io](https://hunter.io) (VERIFY) to find and verify the pattern for their company domain. These tools execute a step; they do not do the thinking for you.

Write the ten names down with one line of evidence beside each. A list of plausible titles with no evidence produces no replies.

## What should I say in cold outreach?

Send under 80 words that names one specific problem, shows you saw the evidence, and asks for a short call to watch them do the task. Do not ask them to sign up, start a trial, or get a demo.

The message I would send looks like this: "Hi [Name], I saw your post in [community] about exporting vendor invoices by hand. I built a small tool that turns that export into a posted journal entry. I am looking for ten people who run this today. Could I watch you handle one real example for 15 minutes? I will leave you with a working setup either way."

No link in the first message. The ask is small and specific. If you sold to finance teams, change the evidence and the job: "I saw your job post for a staff accountant who reconciles cash daily. I built something that does the match and flags breaks."

Send 20 of these over a week and expect a handful of replies. If none return, change one thing: the target, the evidence, the job, or the ask.

## How do I follow up without being annoying?

Add context each time, and stop after two follow-ups. A follow-up is not "just checking in." It should make the reply easier.

First follow-up, two days later: "If 15 minutes is too much, I recorded a 90-second screen share of the tool handling the exact case you described. Does it match how you do it?" Use [Loom](https://www.loom.com) (VERIFY) to record that screen share and send the link.

Second follow-up, five days after that: "I will leave this here. If the timing changes, reply and I can set you up." That line preserves the relationship and stops the sequence. No guilt, no fake urgency.

If they reply no, ask what they did the last time the job came up instead. That answer is worth more than the call.

## How do I handle the first sales call?

Ask them to show you their current process before you show the app. The call is an interview, not a demo.

Start with: "Before I show you anything, can you walk me through the last time this task came up?" Have them share their screen. Keep quiet. Watch for the sigh, the third tab, and the thing they type twice. That is the part your app must remove.

Then offer a guided setup on their next real task. Say: "Can I set you up to run the next version of this in the app while I watch?" Open the app, configure it with their real data, and let them click. Fix what breaks as it breaks. Do not tour features. The buyer does not need to know how it works; they need to see the result.

End with two questions: "When is the next time this task comes up?" and "What would a good result look like for you?" Write the answer down in their words.

## How do I ask for the sale without feeling pushy?

Ask after one real result, in the buyer's words, with a price and a link. You do not pitch. You say: "You said this took ten minutes instead of forty. If you want to keep doing that, it is $49 a month. I will send a link." Then you wait.

Use [Stripe Payment Links](https://docs.stripe.com/payment-links) (VERIFY) to create the product, set the price, and get a checkout URL. Send it in the same message as the ask. Count payments that clear, not people who say yes.

Price under what the current task costs them. If the task costs their team $500 in labor a month, $49 is small. If they ask for a discount, ask which part of the result did not happen. That is a product answer, not a sales answer.

## How do I sell SaaS without AI features?

Sell the result, not the stack. If your product does not use AI, do not apologize for it. Buyers rarely ask for AI unless they are buying AI. They ask whether the task becomes faster, cheaper, or less error-prone.

Your message says what they stop doing by hand, not what technology makes it happen. If someone asks how it works, answer in one sentence and return to the result. "It reads the fields you already export and matches on invoice number and amount." Then ask about their current process. The absence of AI is only an objection if you make it one.

## Should I focus on current users or continue to do outreach and scale?

Do both, but in a fixed order. Current users tell you whether the product works. Outreach tells you whether there are more people with the same problem. The mistake is using one to avoid the other.

Use current users for evidence and wording. Ask one of them: "What did you try before this, and what does it save you now?" Put that sentence in your outreach. Then send the next batch. When a reply raises a new objection, take that objection back to your current users and ask how they would phrase the answer.

The split is ordinary work: two hours on outreach, two hours on current users. Do not spend the whole day on either. If you have no current users, outreach is the only source of them.

## How do I know if my outreach is working?

Count four things by hand: contacts reached, replies, calls, and payments. Do not measure open rates or impressions. A small number of real replies tells you more than a large number of silent views.

A rough expectation: 20 messages produce four or five replies and one or two calls. That is enough to learn. Track it in [Google Sheets](https://sheets.google.com) (VERIFY) with columns for name, evidence, date, reply, call, result, and paid.

If the right people are not replying, the list or the message is wrong. If replies happen but calls do not, your ask is too heavy. If calls happen but no one runs a real task, the first five minutes are wrong. If they run the task and do not pay, the price is off or the buyer is wrong. Change one thing at a time.

## What should I do this week?

Write the ten names today, with one line of evidence beside each, and send the first ten messages tomorrow. Record a 90-second Loom for the first follow-up. Take every call that comes back, watch, and offer to sit with them on the next real task. When someone gets the result and comes back on their own, send the price and the Stripe link the same day. By Friday you will know which part of the sequence is broken, which is more than another week of building will tell you.

# How do I get first users for a vibe-coded product without an audience?

Your app works. The page has been live for a month, the signup count reads one, and that one is a friend.

Here is the answer; the rest is the mechanism. You get first users by finding ten specific people who already do the job your app does, by hand, and asking each of them to let you watch. Do that before you touch the landing page again or post anywhere. It feels impossible because you are trying to be found, and nobody is looking for you yet.

## Why is nobody signing up for my app?

Because nobody knows it exists, and the few who found it could not tell in five seconds whether it was for them.

The first problem is arithmetic. A new site with no links pointing at it gets a handful of visits a day, and most of those are you.

The second is the promise. A vibe-coded app is cheap to widen and expensive to explain, so the homepage ends up promising to "manage your whole workflow," and the visitor with one annoying task cannot find it in there. A promise that covers everything cannot be priced, policed or sold.

## Who should my first users be?

One narrow group that does the job by hand today and that you can reach by name. Ten people, not a market.

"By hand today" is the filter that matters. Someone with a spreadsheet, a group chat or a paper form for this job has already proven the problem is worth their time. Someone who says the idea sounds cool has proven nothing. Someone running the job through a group chat is already paying for it in hours.

Write the ten names down. If you cannot name ten, narrow the group until it has an address, or find a better place to reach the people in it. Look where they ask about the job: forum threads, a trade group, a services page that lists the task. One line of evidence beside each name; a list of plausible names produces nothing.

## How do I reach early users when I have no audience?

One message at a time, asking to watch, not asking them to try the app. Fifteen minutes and a screen share is a real cost. Keep the ask that small and that specific, and say what they get for it: the first version of a tool built around the way they actually work, and a say in what it does. "Try my app" asks for an hour and offers nothing.

The message I would send is under 80 words. It names the evidence that they do the job, says I am building something for exactly that, and asks for 15 minutes to watch them handle one real case the way they do it now. It says what is in it for them. No link. On the call they share their screen and you keep quiet. Notice the sigh, the third tab and the thing they type twice: that is your product, and their words while doing it are your homepage.

Send 20 of those messages over a week and expect four or five replies, as a rough expectation rather than a test. If fewer come back, the cause can be the targeting, the timing, whether they trust a stranger, or the ask itself. Change one of those and send another 20 before you conclude anything about demand.

## How do I turn an interview into a first user?

Offer a guided trial on their next real task, agree up front on what a good outcome looks like, then step back and see whether they come back to it without you.

At the end of the call, ask what the next real instance of this job is and when it lands. Offer to sit with them for that one: you set the app up, they do the task in it, you fix what breaks as it breaks. Before you start, say out loud what success would be, in their terms: "the checklist is right without you editing it," or "this takes under ten minutes." They will tell you if that is the wrong bar, and that is worth knowing before you build toward it.

Then leave. A week later, check whether they opened it on their own and ran a second task through it. That unaided second use is the line between a person who was polite on a call and a user. If they did not come back, ask what they did the next time the job came up instead. The answer is usually more useful than any feature request.

## Should I charge my first users or make it free?

Charge, at a price that is small next to what the job costs them, and ask once someone has a real result and has come back for a second use without your help. Free signups do not prove anyone would pay, though repeat use can still prove the thing is useful.

Price is a question, not a setting. After that second use, you say: "That took four minutes instead of forty, twice. If you want to keep doing that, it is $29 a month. Here is the link." Then you say nothing. Count the payments that clear, not the people who said they would.

The comparison is what the job costs them now: the hours, the missed deadline or the freelancer they were paying. Price under that and above zero.

Make the checkout with [Stripe Payment Links](https://docs.stripe.com/payment-links): create a product, set the price, get a URL, no code in your app. Send it in the same message as the ask. "I would probably pay" with no link in front of it is a compliment, not a customer.

## How do I know if it is working?

Count four things by hand: how many of the right people saw the app, how many got one real result, how many came back on their own, and how many paid.

Zero signups from ten visits is too little evidence to judge, which is why you count people you can name rather than sessions. Still, install one thing so you know whether anyone came. [umami](https://github.com/umami-software/umami) is lightweight, privacy-friendly analytics that counts visits and the few events you tell it to. If you want to see what people did, [PostHog](https://posthog.com) is open source ([PostHog/posthog](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog)) and tracks those events as a funnel and records sessions; watch five recordings of people who landed and left. Where they stall is usually where your onboarding assumes they know something they do not.

For the people who got in and stopped, [formbricks](https://github.com/formbricks/formbricks) puts a one-question survey inside your app at the step they abandon. Ask "what were you hoping this would do?" and nothing else. The answers are your next homepage draft.

When the counts come back, change one thing. If the right people are not showing up, the list or the message is wrong. If they show up and do not get the result, the first five minutes of your app are wrong. If they get the result and do not come back, the job is rarer or smaller than you thought. If they come back and will not pay, the price is off or the promise is aimed at the wrong person. Each is a different week of work, and mixing them is how a month disappears.

## How many users is enough to know I am on the right track?

Fewer than you think, and it is a shape rather than a number. You are on the right track when people you did not sit with are running real work through the app on their own, and some of them have paid. Three of those tell you more than three hundred signups from a launch post who never opened it again.

Nobody can give you a count that certifies it, and anyone who does is guessing. What you can watch is whether each round of ten conversations produces more of those people than the last one. When it does, keep going. When it does not, go back to the four counts and change one thing.

## What should I do this week?

Write the ten names today, with the evidence beside each. Send the first five messages tomorrow. Take every call that comes back, watch, and end each one by offering to sit with them on the next real task. Note who comes back on their own; when one of them does, send the price and the link the same day. By Friday you will know something you do not know now, which is more than the last month of feature work told you.
