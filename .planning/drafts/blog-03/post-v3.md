# How do I sell my app when I am a builder, not a salesperson?

Your app works, and every time you think about selling it you add a feature instead. Every sales guide assumes a deck, a pitch, and a personality you do not have.

Here is the answer; the rest is the mechanism. Selling is not a personality. It is a sequence you run by hand: ten people who do the job today, one message about a problem they have, and a call where they do the talking. Then a price said out loud before the setup, and a link sent the day they get a result. You need a list, a message, two follow-ups, a price, and four numbers.

## Why can't I sell my product even though it works?

Because you are selling a product, and nobody buys a product. They buy a change. The product is too big to explain and too easy to ignore; the change is specific. Before this, Monday morning went to copying invoices by hand; after this, the export is posted before the coffee is done. If you cannot say the change in one sentence, you are not ready to sell yet.

The other reason is a blurry target. Most builders who cannot sell have a working product aimed at "small teams," "busy operators," or "anyone who invoices." A buyer cannot find themselves in that. Aim at one person who does the job by hand today and has left public evidence of it.

## Who should I contact first?

Ten people who already do the job your app replaces and whose evidence you can point to.

Evidence lives in public places: a forum thread asking how to automate the task, a job post for a person to run it, a template someone shared to manage it. Write the ten names down with one line of evidence beside each. If there is no public evidence yet, narrow the role, ask in the trade group where the job gets discussed, or talk to the people who hire for it. A job post is proof that someone already pays for the task by hand.

When the evidence names a company but not an address, [Hunter](https://hunter.io) finds the addresses in use at that domain and checks whether one is valid; the free plan covers a list this short. Do not buy a list; a thousand titles with no evidence is a thousand messages nobody answers.

## What should I say in cold outreach?

A message under 80 words that names one problem they have written about, says you built something for exactly that, and asks to watch them do the task. Do not ask them to sign up, start a trial, or take a demo.

Here is the message I would send: "Hi [Name], I saw your post in [community] about exporting vendor invoices by hand. I built a small tool that turns that export into a posted journal entry. I am looking for ten people who run this today. Could I watch you handle one real example for 15 minutes? I will leave you with a working setup either way."

No link, no deck, no calendar page.

## How do I follow up without being annoying?

Add something each time, and stop after two. "Just checking in" adds nothing; a follow-up that makes the reply easier is not annoying.

First follow-up, two days later: "If 15 minutes is too much, I recorded a 90-second screen share of the tool handling the case you described. Does it match how you do it?" Record it with [Loom](https://www.loom.com), which captures your screen and camera and gives you a link to send; there is a free plan.

Second follow-up, five days after that: "I will leave this here. If the timing changes, reply and I can set you up."

A clear no ends it; thank them and stop. If the no comes with a reason or a "not right now," one question is fair: what did you do the last time this came up? That answer is worth more than the call.

## How do I handle the first sales call?

Ask them to show you the job before you show them the app. The call is an interview, and the buyer gives the demo.

Open with: "Before I show you anything, can you walk me through the last time this came up?" Have them share their screen and stay quiet. Write down where they slow down, what they copy from one window to another, and what they check twice. That is the part your app has to remove.

Then offer a guided setup on their next real task: "Can I set you up to run the next one in the app while I watch?" Do not tour the features; the buyer needs to see the result, not how it works. End with two questions and write the answers down in their words: "When is the next time this comes up?" and "What would a good result look like?"

## When should I mention the price?

Before they give you an hour for the guided setup, not after. Say it on the first call, in one sentence, when they ask what happens next: "If it works for you, it is $49 a month. The setup costs you nothing but the hour."

One result is not always a yes. A freelancer can pay the same afternoon; a team with a manager and a procurement form cannot, and a link pushed at them ends the conversation. When the buyer is a company, agree the next step instead: who else needs to see the result, and by when.

## How do I ask for the sale without feeling pushy?

After one real result, in the buyer's words, with the price they already heard and the link in the same message. "You said this took ten minutes instead of forty. If you want to keep doing that, it is the $49 a month I mentioned. Here is the link." Then stop typing.

Make the link with [Stripe Payment Links](https://docs.stripe.com/payment-links): create a product, set the price, get a URL, no code in your app. Send it in the same message as the ask.

Price against what the job costs them by hand. If they ask for a discount, find out what is behind it before you answer: a budget that will not stretch, doubt that the result will hold, or a company that buys on yearly terms. Each has a different answer, and only the second is about the product. If they need to think about it, ask what is missing from the decision. A missing fact gets the smallest proof you have; a missing feature gets scope and a purchase agreed before you build a line of it. Custom work built on a maybe is unpaid work.

## How do I sell SaaS without AI features?

Sell the result and never mention the stack. Buyers rarely ask for AI unless AI is what they are buying; they ask whether the task gets faster, cheaper, or less error-prone.

If someone asks how it works, answer in one sentence and return to their process: "It reads the fields you already export and matches on invoice number and amount." Apologizing for the absence of AI hands the buyer an objection they did not have.

## Should I focus on current users or keep doing outreach?

Both, and never one as an excuse to skip the other. A builder with three users will polish for them all month to avoid sending an email; a builder with none will send a hundred emails to avoid learning the product is not finished.

Use current users for wording and evidence. Ask one of them: "What did you try before this, and what does it save you now?" Put that sentence in the next batch of outreach.

## How do I know if my outreach is working?

Count four things by hand: people reached, replies, calls, and payments. Open rates are a delivery check, not a selling number: if nobody opens, the subject line or the sending domain needs fixing. Keep the four in one spreadsheet with a row per person: name, evidence, date sent, reply, call, result, paid.

Each gap between the four numbers is a place to start looking, not a verdict. No replies usually means the list or the message. Replies but no calls points at the ask. Calls but no setups suggests the call ran as a demo, though it can also mean the task comes up less often than they said. Setups but no payments points at the price or the buyer. Change one thing, send the next batch, and see whether the number moves.

## What should I do this week?

Write the ten names today, each with a line of evidence, and send the first ten messages tomorrow. Take every call, watch, say the price out loud, and end each one by offering to sit with them on the next real task. When someone gets a result, send the link the same day if they can decide alone, or agree the next step if they cannot. By Friday you will have four numbers, and the biggest drop between them is next week's work. No feature you could ship in the same five days would tell you that.
