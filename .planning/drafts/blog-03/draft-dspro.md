# How do I sell my app when I am a builder, not a salesperson?

Your app works. People who use it get a result. The problem is that the people who should use it do not know it exists.

Here is the answer; the rest is the mechanism. You sell by picking ten specific buyers, sending a message about one problem they already recognize, and asking for a short call. You do not need a sales personality. You need a list, a message, a follow-up sequence, and four numbers.

## Why can't I sell my product even though it works?

Because you are selling a product instead of a change. The product is too big to explain and too easy to ignore. The change is specific: before this, you spent Monday morning copying invoices by hand; after this, the export produces them before you finish coffee. If you cannot say the change in one sentence, you are not ready to sell yet.

Most builders who cannot sell have a working product aimed at a blurry person. They write to "small teams" or "busy operators." A buyer cannot find themselves in that. You need one person who does the job by hand today and has public evidence of it. That evidence is the doorway.

## Who should I contact first?

People who already do the job your app replaces, and who show the cost of doing it by hand. Ten names, not a market.

Look for evidence in public places: a forum thread asking how to automate the task, a job post for a person to run it, a template shared to manage it, or a support ticket that keeps repeating. If you cannot find ten people with that evidence, narrow the role or change the job you are targeting.

For a business buyer, use [LinkedIn Sales Navigator](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/sales-navigator) (VERIFY) to filter by title, company size, and industry. For email addresses, use [Hunter.io](https://hunter.io) (VERIFY) to find and verify the pattern for their company domain. These tools execute a step; they do not do the thinking for you.

Write the ten names down with one line of evidence beside each. A list of plausible titles with no evidence produces no replies.

## What should I say in cold outreach?

Send under 80 words that names one specific problem, shows you saw the evidence, and asks for a short call to watch them do the task. Do not ask them to sign up, start a trial, or get a demo.

The message I would send looks like this: "Hi [Name], I saw your post in [community] about exporting vendor invoices by hand. I built a small tool that turns that export into a posted journal entry. I am looking for ten people who run this today. Could I watch you handle one real example for 15 minutes? I will leave you with a working setup either way."

No link in the first message. The ask is small and specific. If you sold to finance teams, change the evidence and the job: "I saw your job post for a staff accountant who reconciles cash daily. I built something that does the match and flags breaks."

Send 20 of these over a week and expect a handful of replies. If none return, change one thing: the target, the evidence, the job, or the ask.

## How do I follow up without being annoying?

Add context each time, and stop after two follow-ups. A follow-up is not "just checking in." It should make the reply easier.

First follow-up, two days later: "If 15 minutes is too much, I recorded a 90-second screen share of the tool handling the exact case you described. Does it match how you do it?" Use [Loom](https://www.loom.com) (VERIFY) to record that screen share and send the link.

Second follow-up, five days after that: "I will leave this here. If the timing changes, reply and I can set you up." That line preserves the relationship and stops the sequence. No guilt, no fake urgency.

If they reply no, ask what they did the last time the job came up instead. That answer is worth more than the call.

## How do I handle the first sales call?

Ask them to show you their current process before you show the app. The call is an interview, not a demo.

Start with: "Before I show you anything, can you walk me through the last time this task came up?" Have them share their screen. Keep quiet. Watch for the sigh, the third tab, and the thing they type twice. That is the part your app must remove.

Then offer a guided setup on their next real task. Say: "Can I set you up to run the next version of this in the app while I watch?" Open the app, configure it with their real data, and let them click. Fix what breaks as it breaks. Do not tour features. The buyer does not need to know how it works; they need to see the result.

End with two questions: "When is the next time this task comes up?" and "What would a good result look like for you?" Write the answer down in their words.

## How do I ask for the sale without feeling pushy?

Ask after one real result, in the buyer's words, with a price and a link. You do not pitch. You say: "You said this took ten minutes instead of forty. If you want to keep doing that, it is $49 a month. I will send a link." Then you wait.

Use [Stripe Payment Links](https://docs.stripe.com/payment-links) (VERIFY) to create the product, set the price, and get a checkout URL. Send it in the same message as the ask. Count payments that clear, not people who say yes.

Price under what the current task costs them. If the task costs their team $500 in labor a month, $49 is small. If they ask for a discount, ask which part of the result did not happen. That is a product answer, not a sales answer.

## How do I sell SaaS without AI features?

Sell the result, not the stack. If your product does not use AI, do not apologize for it. Buyers rarely ask for AI unless they are buying AI. They ask whether the task becomes faster, cheaper, or less error-prone.

Your message says what they stop doing by hand, not what technology makes it happen. If someone asks how it works, answer in one sentence and return to the result. "It reads the fields you already export and matches on invoice number and amount." Then ask about their current process. The absence of AI is only an objection if you make it one.

## Should I focus on current users or continue to do outreach and scale?

Do both, but in a fixed order. Current users tell you whether the product works. Outreach tells you whether there are more people with the same problem. The mistake is using one to avoid the other.

Use current users for evidence and wording. Ask one of them: "What did you try before this, and what does it save you now?" Put that sentence in your outreach. Then send the next batch. When a reply raises a new objection, take that objection back to your current users and ask how they would phrase the answer.

The split is ordinary work: two hours on outreach, two hours on current users. Do not spend the whole day on either. If you have no current users, outreach is the only source of them.

## How do I know if my outreach is working?

Count four things by hand: contacts reached, replies, calls, and payments. Do not measure open rates or impressions. A small number of real replies tells you more than a large number of silent views.

A rough expectation: 20 messages produce four or five replies and one or two calls. That is enough to learn. Track it in [Google Sheets](https://sheets.google.com) (VERIFY) with columns for name, evidence, date, reply, call, result, and paid.

If the right people are not replying, the list or the message is wrong. If replies happen but calls do not, your ask is too heavy. If calls happen but no one runs a real task, the first five minutes are wrong. If they run the task and do not pay, the price is off or the buyer is wrong. Change one thing at a time.

## What should I do this week?

Write the ten names today, with one line of evidence beside each, and send the first ten messages tomorrow. Record a 90-second Loom for the first follow-up. Take every call that comes back, watch, and offer to sit with them on the next real task. When someone gets the result and comes back on their own, send the price and the Stripe link the same day. By Friday you will know which part of the sequence is broken, which is more than another week of building will tell you.
