# Why isn't my landing page converting?

The page is live, the layout is clean, and people land on it and leave. You have rewritten it three times and still cannot say in one sentence what it does.

That sentence is the problem, not the layout. A landing page converts when a stranger can tell in five seconds who it is for, what it takes away, and what to do next. The rest of this post is how to write that sentence, test it on five people, and find the one line where the page loses everyone else.

## Why do visitors leave without signing up?

Because the page explains the product and the visitor came with a problem. A stranger gives it five seconds and asks one thing: is this for me? If the first line answers with a feature, they go.

You know the product inside out, so the page reads clearly to you. The visitor knows nothing, and the first line has to carry all of that context on its own. "AI-powered workflow automation for invoices" is a feature. "Get paid on time without the awkward email" is a problem and a result. The first line is about them, not about the product, and every rewrite that starts from the product will fail the same way.

## How do I say what my app does in one sentence?

Fill three slots: the person, the situation, the result. "For freelancers who chase late invoices, this sends a reminder that sounds like you and gets the invoice paid." That sentence skips most of the features. It names who it is for, what hurts, and what changes.

Write it before you touch anything else on the page. Specific beats big. "Manage your whole client cycle" promises everything and describes nobody, so nobody sees themselves in it. If the sentence sounds too small for the product you built, that is usually the sign it has finally become sharp enough to sell.

## What should the headline promise?

The result, in the visitor's words, with no product name and no category in it. The headline is the one sentence cut down to the part they came for. "Get paid on time without the awkward email" is a headline. "Automated invoice reminders for freelancers" is a catalogue entry.

The line under it names the person and the mechanism: "For freelancers who chase late invoices. It sends the follow-up in your voice, so you stay friendly and still get paid." That pair is the whole promise. Everything below exists to prove it.

## What should go below the headline?

Proof of the promise, then the three steps, then the one button. A visitor who believes the headline asks three things in order: is this really for me, what do I have to do, and what happens first?

Proof is a picture of the result, not a list of features: a screenshot of the reminder as a client receives it, or a short clip of one going out. Then one line for the steps: "Connect your invoicing tool, pick one reminder template, and the follow-up goes out on its own." Then the button. Anything that does not answer one of the three questions belongs further down the page or on another one.

## How do I find the words my visitors already use?

Listen to one person describe the problem in their own terms and copy the phrasing without cleaning it up. If a freelancer says "I hate the awkward follow-up email," the page says "the awkward follow-up email," not "streamlined client communication." A visitor recognizes their own sentence faster than any sentence you could write for them.

Two places hold those words. The first is a five-minute call with the kind of person you want: ask them to describe the last time the problem happened, and write down the exact phrases. The second is the email or support message in which someone first asked for the thing you built. That request, more or less as written, is often the best headline you will get.

## How do I test whether my landing page is clear?

Run a five-second test with five people who have never seen the page. Show it for five seconds, close it, and ask one question: what do you think this is for? A good answer contains the person and the result. "Something about invoices" is a miss. "It chases late invoices for freelancers" is a hit.

Do this before any design work. If three of the five describe the problem you meant, the copy holds. If they describe something else, change the headline first and run it again. Friends who know what you built cannot take this test, because they will read the product into the page for you.

## How do I know where the page loses people?

Watch recordings of visitors who landed and left. [Microsoft Clarity](https://clarity.microsoft.com) is free, and it records sessions and draws heatmaps of where people click, scroll and drop off. Install it, give it a few days of traffic, and watch five recordings of people who never reached the button. Find the point where the scrolling stops or the cursor drifts back to the top. That is where the page stopped answering the next question.

Then fix the line at that point, not the whole section. Maybe the headline worked and the "how it works" line introduced a term they did not know. Maybe they reached the price before they knew what they were paying for. The recording tells you where. The words still tell you why.

## Should I change the words or the design first?

Words first. The clearest button on the internet cannot rescue a headline that says "workflow automation platform." People leave because they cannot see themselves on the page, not because it lacks polish.

Change three things: the headline, the line under it, and the label on the main button. The button names the next step the person takes, not "Submit" and not "Get started." For the invoice example it reads "Send my first reminder." If the page is readable on a phone and the button is easy to tap, leave the design alone until the new words have been tested.

## How do I know if the landing page is converting?

Count the one action, not the visits. Pick a single goal that matches the promise: a signup, a started trial, or a question sent. If the promise is "get paid on time," the goal is the click that sets up the first reminder, and nothing else on the page counts. Any analytics tool can count one event; if you run none, [umami](https://github.com/umami-software/umami) is lightweight, privacy-friendly analytics that counts visits and the few events you tell it to.

Then check what people typed to find you. [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console/about) shows which queries put your page in Google results, with impressions and clicks for each. If the query is "invoice reminder for freelancers" and the headline says "client communication made simple," the search and the page disagree, and the search is right. Those queries are headline material.

## What should I do this week?

Write the one sentence today: person, situation, result. Put the result in the headline and the person in the line under it. Run the five-second test with five strangers tomorrow. Install Clarity, and by Thursday watch five recordings of people who left. Change the one line where they stopped, then run the five-second test again. By Friday you will know whether the page says what you meant, which is more than the last three rewrites told you.

# How do I sell my app when I am a builder, not a salesperson?

Your app works, and every time you think about selling it you add a feature instead. Every sales guide assumes a deck, a pitch, and a personality you do not have.

Here is the answer; the rest is the mechanism. Selling is not a personality. It is a sequence you run by hand: ten people who do the job today, one message about a problem they have, a call where they do the talking, and a price sent the day they get a result. You need a list, a message, two follow-ups, and four numbers.

## Why can't I sell my product even though it works?

Because you are selling a product, and nobody buys a product. They buy a change. The product is too big to explain and too easy to ignore; the change is specific. Before this, Monday morning went to copying invoices by hand; after this, the export is posted before the coffee is done. If you cannot say the change in one sentence, you are not ready to sell yet.

The other reason is a blurry target. Most builders who cannot sell have a working product aimed at "small teams," "busy operators," or "anyone who invoices." A buyer cannot find themselves in that. Aim at one person who does the job by hand today and has left public evidence of it.

## Who should I contact first?

Ten people who already do the job your app replaces and whose evidence you can point to.

Evidence lives in public places: a forum thread asking how to automate the task, a job post for a person to run it, a template someone shared to manage it, a complaint in a trade group. Write the ten names down with one line of evidence beside each. If you cannot find ten, narrow the role or change the job you are targeting.

When the evidence names a company but not an address, [Hunter](https://hunter.io) finds the addresses in use at that domain, finds one person's address from their name, and checks whether it is valid; the free plan covers a list this short. Do not buy a list; a thousand titles with no evidence is a thousand messages nobody answers.

## What should I say in cold outreach?

A message under 80 words that names one problem they have written about, says you built something for exactly that, and asks to watch them do the task. Do not ask them to sign up, start a trial, or take a demo.

Here is the message I would send: "Hi [Name], I saw your post in [community] about exporting vendor invoices by hand. I built a small tool that turns that export into a posted journal entry. I am looking for ten people who run this today. Could I watch you handle one real example for 15 minutes? I will leave you with a working setup either way."

No link, no deck, no calendar page. The ask is to watch, and the offer is something they keep.

## How do I follow up without being annoying?

Add something each time, and stop after two. "Just checking in" adds nothing; a follow-up that makes the reply easier is not annoying.

First follow-up, two days later: "If 15 minutes is too much, I recorded a 90-second screen share of the tool handling the case you described. Does it match how you do it?" Record it with [Loom](https://www.loom.com), which captures your screen and camera and gives you a link to send; there is a free plan.

Second follow-up, five days after that: "I will leave this here. If the timing changes, reply and I can set you up." That line ends the sequence without guilt or a fake deadline.

If the reply is a no, ask what they did the last time this came up. That answer is worth more than the call.

## How do I handle the first sales call?

Ask them to show you the job before you show them the app. The call is an interview, and the buyer gives the demo.

Open with: "Before I show you anything, can you walk me through the last time this came up?" Have them share their screen and stay quiet. Write down where they slow down, what they copy from one window to another, and what they check twice. That is the part your app has to remove.

Then offer a guided setup on their next real task: "Can I set you up to run the next one in the app while I watch?" Do not tour the features; the buyer needs to see the result, not how it works. End with two questions and write the answers down in their words: "When is the next time this comes up?" and "What would a good result look like?"

## How do I ask for the sale without feeling pushy?

Ask after one real result, in the buyer's words, with the price and the link in the same message. That is the whole close. "You said this took ten minutes instead of forty. If you want to keep doing that, it is $49 a month. Here is the link." Then stop typing.

Make the link with [Stripe Payment Links](https://docs.stripe.com/payment-links): create a product, set the price, get a URL, no code in your app. Send it in the same message, because a yes with no link in front of it does not stay a yes for long.

Price against what the job costs them now. If the task eats a day of someone's month, $49 is small next to it. If they ask for a discount, ask which part of the result did not happen. That turns a haggle into a product conversation, which a builder can win. If they say they need to think about it, ask what they would need to see to decide, build that one thing, and send it. That is the only follow-up that moves a sale.

## How do I sell SaaS without AI features?

Sell the result and never mention the stack. Buyers rarely ask for AI unless AI is what they are buying. They ask whether the task gets faster, cheaper, or less error-prone.

Your message says what they stop doing by hand. If someone asks how it works, answer in one sentence and return to their process: "It reads the fields you already export and matches on invoice number and amount." Then ask what they do today when a match fails. The absence of AI is an objection only if you raise it, and apologizing for it hands the buyer an objection they did not have.

## Should I focus on current users or keep doing outreach?

Both, and never one as an excuse to skip the other. Current users tell you whether the product works. Outreach tells you whether more people have the same problem. A builder with three users will polish for them all month to avoid sending an email; a builder with none will send a hundred emails to avoid learning the product is not finished.

Use current users for wording and evidence. Ask one of them: "What did you try before this, and what does it save you now?" Put that sentence in the next batch of outreach. When a reply raises an objection you have not heard, take it back to a current user and ask how they would answer it. Two hours on outreach and two on current users beats a whole day on either.

## How do I know if my outreach is working?

Count four things by hand: people reached, replies, calls, and payments. Open rates and impressions are not on the list; a few real replies tell you more than a thousand silent views. Keep the four in one spreadsheet with a row per person: name, evidence, date sent, reply, call, result, paid.

Each gap between the four numbers points at a different fix. No replies means the list or the message. Replies but no calls means the ask is too heavy. Calls but no setup means you demoed instead of watching. Setups but no payments means the price or the buyer. Change one thing, send the next batch, and read the numbers again.

## What should I do this week?

Write the ten names today, each with a line of evidence, and send the first ten messages tomorrow. Record the 90-second Loom before the first follow-up is due. Take every call, watch, and end each one by offering to sit with them on the next real task. When someone gets a result, send the price and the Stripe link the same day, in their words. By Friday you will have four numbers, and the biggest drop between them is next week's work. No feature you could ship in the same five days would tell you that.
